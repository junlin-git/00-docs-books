## 文章 31：The Silk Road: Connecting Civilizations

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约330词 | **主题**：丝绸之路

---

Long before aeroplanes, container ships, or the internet, human civilisations found ways to connect across vast distances. The Silk Road — that ancient network of trade routes linking China to the Mediterranean — was one of the most remarkable examples of cross-cultural exchange in human history.

The name "Silk Road" was coined by the 19th-century German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, though the routes themselves had been in use for over a millennium before he named them. At its peak, between roughly 200 BCE and 1400 CE, the network stretched approximately 6,400 kilometres — from the ancient capitals of China, through Central Asia, Persia, and the Arabian Peninsula, to the ports of the Mediterranean.

Silk — China's most prized export — gave the routes their name, but the range of goods that travelled along them was extraordinarily diverse: spices, glass, cotton, precious metals, horses, and exotic animals moved in both directions. Knowledge travelled too: agricultural techniques, papermaking, gunpowder, the compass, and sophisticated mathematical systems spread westward along these routes, while Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity spread eastward.

Crucially, the Silk Road was not a single path but a web of interconnecting routes — overland across the Central Asian steppe, through mountain passes like the Khyber Pass, and maritime routes across the Indian Ocean. Travellers rarely made the entire journey; goods typically passed through many hands as they moved from market to market.

The Silk Road's significance extends beyond economics. It was a conduit for the exchange of ideas, religions, technologies, and artistic traditions that profoundly shaped every civilisation it touched. Chinese motifs appeared in Persian carpets; Roman glassware reached Chinese tombs; Buddhist art synthesised influences from Greece, India, and China into something entirely new.

The decline of the overland Silk Road came with the opening of direct maritime routes to Asia by European explorers in the 15th and 16th centuries. Yet its legacy lives on — in the languages, art, food, and philosophies that it helped blend and distribute across the world.

## 文章 32：The Renaissance: Rebirth of Human Potential

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约320词 | **主题**：文艺复兴

---

The Renaissance — from the French word meaning "rebirth" — was one of the most transformative periods in European cultural history. Beginning in the Italian city-states of the 14th century and spreading across Europe over the next two centuries, it marked a decisive shift from the medieval worldview towards one that placed human beings, rather than God alone, at the centre of inquiry.

Renaissance humanism drew its inspiration from the rediscovery of classical Greek and Roman texts, which had been preserved in the libraries of Islamic scholars and Byzantine monasteries. These texts — the works of Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, and Vitruvius — offered a vision of human achievement and rational inquiry that challenged the theological certainties of medieval scholarship.

The arts flourished as never before. Leonardo da Vinci exemplified the Renaissance ideal of the "universal man" (uomo universale) — artist, scientist, engineer, and philosopher in one. Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and Botticelli's Birth of Venus explored human beauty and classical mythology with unprecedented technical mastery. In literature, Dante, Petrarch, and later Shakespeare shaped the literary languages and imaginative traditions of nations.

The Renaissance also accelerated scientific inquiry. Copernicus proposed that the Earth orbited the Sun rather than the reverse, challenging centuries of geocentric cosmology. Galileo's telescope observations provided empirical evidence that further undermined the Ptolemaic model. These early steps laid the groundwork for the Scientific Revolution.

The printing press, invented by Gutenberg around 1440, was a technological enabler of the Renaissance's spread. By making books cheaper and more widely available, it democratised access to knowledge in a way unprecedented in human history — dramatically accelerating the spread of humanist ideas and the challenges to established authority they contained.

The Renaissance did not emerge in a vacuum. It required relative political stability, the wealth of Italian merchant cities, and the particular circumstances of a Europe recovering from plague and ready to reimagine itself.

## 文章 33：The Impact of World War I on Society

**难度**：B2-C1 | **字数**：约340词 | **主题**：一战对社会的影响

---

When Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo in June 1914, few could have predicted that the spark would ignite a war that would claim an estimated 20 million lives and reshape the entire political map of the world.

The First World War (1914-1918) was unlike any previous conflict in its industrial scale and technological character. Machine guns, artillery, poison gas, tanks, and aircraft transformed warfare into something unprecedented in its destructiveness. The trenches of the Western Front — stretching from Belgium to Switzerland — became symbols of industrial-age slaughter: millions of soldiers lived and died in conditions of horrifying squalor and danger.

The social impact of the war was equally transformative. In many combatant nations, women entered the workforce in large numbers to replace men serving at the front — in factories, offices, transport, and agriculture. This economic contribution strengthened the political case for women's suffrage, which was granted to women in the United Kingdom in 1918 (initially only to women over 30) and in the United States in 1920.

The psychological toll was immense. Millions of veterans returned from the front with what we now call post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), then known as "shell shock." The trauma was not limited to those who fought — entire communities grieved the catastrophic loss of young men. The disillusionment of a generation produced some of the most powerful literature in the English language: the poetry of Wilfred Owen and Siegfried Sassoon, the novels of Erich Maria Remarque and Ernest Hemingway.

The political consequences proved no less seismic. The collapse of the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman empires reshaped the map of Europe and the Middle East. The harsh terms of the Treaty of Versailles, which blamed Germany entirely for the war and imposed crippling reparations, created the conditions of resentment and economic instability that would eventually give rise to Adolf Hitler and the Second World War.

## 文章 34：The Cultural Revolution in China

**难度**：C1 | **字数**：约330词 | **主题**：中国文化大革命

---

The Cultural Revolution (文化大革命, 1966-1976) was one of the most radical and destructive political movements of the 20th century — a decade-long campaign launched by Mao Zedong that sought to purge Chinese society of what he termed "capitalist" and "traditional" elements and to reinforce communist ideology.

Launched with the mobilisation of millions of young people as "Red Guards," the movement targeted intellectuals, teachers, party officials, religious figures, and anyone suspected of insufficient revolutionary commitment. Schools and universities were closed. Historical monuments, temples, books, and artworks were destroyed in a frenzied assault on China's cultural heritage. Millions were persecuted, imprisoned, sent to labour camps for "re-education," or killed. China's educated class was systematically decimated.

The economic disruption was profound. Agricultural and industrial production fell. China's scientific and technological development was set back by at least a generation, as experts and researchers were condemned and silenced.

The Cultural Revolution's impact on Chinese society was traumatic and enduring. Families were torn apart when children denounced their parents, students denounced their teachers. Trust between people collapsed under the weight of constant surveillance and ideological pressure. An entire generation lost their formative years to political chaos rather than education.

The official verdict on the Cultural Revolution in China today acknowledges it as a period of serious error — the 1981 Party Resolution described it as a catastrophe for the Party and state. However, public discussion of this period remains sensitive, and a full historical reckoning with its causes and consequences has not occurred.

Understanding the Cultural Revolution is essential to understanding contemporary China — the generation that experienced it includes many of China's current leaders. Its memory shapes attitudes towards political stability, rapid change, and the relationship between reform and order that continue to influence Chinese politics today.

## 文章 35：The Harlem Renaissance

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约310词 | **主题**：哈莱姆文艺复兴

---

In the 1920s and 1930s, a neighbourhood in New York City became the cultural capital of Black America. Harlem, in upper Manhattan, was the site of an extraordinary flowering of African American art, literature, music, and intellectual life that came to be known as the Harlem Renaissance.

The movement was preceded by the Great Migration — the mass movement of African Americans from the rural South to Northern cities that accelerated from around 1910 onwards, driven by the promise of economic opportunity and escape from the systematic racism and terror of the Jim Crow South. By 1920, Harlem had become the most densely populated Black neighbourhood in the United States.

Into this concentrated community of talent, energy, and aspiration came writers like Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, and Claude McKay; visual artists like Aaron Douglas; musicians who helped create or shape jazz, blues, and gospel; and intellectuals like W.E.B. Du Bois, whose magazine "The Crisis" became one of the most influential publications in African American history.

The Harlem Renaissance was not merely artistic — it was a profound assertion of Black humanity, dignity, and cultural identity at a time when African Americans faced pervasive legal discrimination, racial violence, and systematic exclusion from the mainstream of American life. Its works challenged stereotypes, celebrated African heritage, and insisted on the full humanity and complexity of Black experience.

The Great Depression of the 1930s brought the Renaissance to a practical end, as economic collapse devastated the community and the patronage networks that had sustained artistic production. But its legacy proved enduring. The writers, artists, and musicians of the Harlem Renaissance shaped the cultural foundations of the Civil Rights Movement and continue to influence American and global culture today.
