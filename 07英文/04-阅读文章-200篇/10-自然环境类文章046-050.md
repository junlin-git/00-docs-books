## 文章 46：Plastic: The Material That Changed and Now Threatens the World

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约320词 | **主题**：塑料污染

---

When Bakelite — the first synthetic plastic — was invented in 1907, it was celebrated as a revolutionary material: lightweight, durable, mouldable, and cheap. Over the following century, plastics transformed virtually every aspect of human life — from packaging to medicine, from electronics to clothing.

The same properties that make plastic so useful also make it a serious environmental problem. Plastic is durable — it does not biodegrade in any meaningful timeframe. A plastic bottle discarded today will persist in the environment for hundreds of years. Since mass plastic production began in the 1950s, approximately 9.2 billion tonnes of plastic have been produced; an estimated 6.9 billion tonnes have become waste.

Much of this waste ends up in the natural environment — in soils, waterways, and the ocean. An estimated 8 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean annually. Here, plastic is broken down by UV radiation and wave action into smaller and smaller particles — microplastics — but it does not disappear. Microplastics have been found in every ocean on Earth, in Arctic snow, in the deepest ocean trenches, in the air above cities, and in human blood.

The impacts on wildlife are devastating. Seabirds, sea turtles, and marine mammals regularly ingest plastic, mistaking it for food. Ghost fishing gear — nets and lines abandoned or lost at sea — continues to catch marine life for years. Smaller plastic particles are now present throughout marine food chains.

Responses are emerging at multiple levels. Single-use plastic bans have been adopted in many countries and cities. Deposit return schemes have dramatically improved plastic bottle recycling rates in several countries. Innovations in biodegradable and compostable materials are advancing. Extended producer responsibility legislation — requiring manufacturers to take responsibility for the end-of-life management of their products — is increasingly being adopted.

But addressing the plastic crisis fundamentally requires producing far less plastic in the first place, not merely managing it better after production.

## 文章 47：Water: The Coming Crisis

**难度**：B2-C1 | **字数**：约330词 | **主题**：全球水危机

---

Water is the most fundamental requirement of human life. Yet fresh water — the water available for drinking, agriculture, and industry — constitutes only about 3% of all water on Earth, and of that, approximately two-thirds is locked in glaciers and ice caps. The accessible fresh water in rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers is limited, unevenly distributed, and under increasing pressure.

Today, approximately 2.2 billion people lack access to safely managed drinking water. Four billion people face severe water scarcity for at least one month of the year. As global population grows and climate change alters precipitation patterns and accelerates glacial melting, the pressure on freshwater resources is intensifying.

Agriculture accounts for approximately 70% of global freshwater withdrawals. The "virtual water" concept captures how much water is embedded in the production of goods: it takes approximately 15,500 litres of water to produce one kilogram of beef, 2,500 litres for one kilogram of rice, and around 130 litres for a single cup of coffee. As global diets have shifted towards more water-intensive foods, agriculture's demand on freshwater systems has grown.

Groundwater depletion is a particular concern. Many of the world's most productive agricultural regions — including large parts of India, China, the United States, and the Middle East — rely on irrigation from aquifers that are being drawn down far faster than they are naturally replenished. When these aquifers run dry, the agricultural systems that depend on them will collapse.

Climate change compounds all these pressures — melting glaciers that feed major river systems, changing rainfall patterns, more frequent droughts and floods. The regions most severely affected will include sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, the Middle East, and parts of South and Southeast Asia.

Addressing the global water crisis requires smarter water pricing, investment in more water-efficient irrigation technologies, improved wastewater treatment and reuse, and international cooperation on transboundary water resources.

## 文章 48：Rewilding: Restoring Nature at Scale

**难度**：C1 | **字数**：约330词 | **主题**：野化与生态修复

---

For most of conservation history, the dominant approach to protecting nature was essentially defensive — establishing protected areas, controlling invasive species, and preventing further habitat destruction. Rewilding represents a fundamentally different, more ambitious vision: actively restoring the ecological processes and wild species that have been lost, allowing natural systems to recover and self-regulate.

The concept draws on a recognition that ecosystems function best not simply as collections of species but as dynamic systems shaped by the interactions between them — particularly the interactions involving large predators and herbivores, which structure vegetation, regulate prey populations, and create conditions for the persistence of many other species.

The most famous rewilding example is the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995, after their absence of 70 years. The wolves' return triggered what ecologists call a "trophic cascade" — a chain of ecological changes that rippled through the entire ecosystem. Wolf predation controlled elk populations, which had been overgrazing riverbank vegetation. With reduced grazing pressure, trees and shrubs recovered along riverbanks, stabilising soils and channels. Beaver populations expanded, creating wetlands. Bird populations increased. The trophic cascade even affected the physical shape of rivers.

In Europe, rewilding projects are reintroducing bison, lynx, and other lost species to landscapes in the UK, Netherlands, and across Eastern Europe. In the UK, "rewilding" pioneer Isabella Tree's Knepp Estate in Sussex — a former conventional farm — has been transformed into a thriving haven for rare species within just a few decades, simply by removing intensive management and allowing natural processes to take over.

Rewilding is not without controversy. Farmers worry about predator attacks on livestock and competing land use priorities. Community consultation and conflict mitigation are essential. But the rewilding movement offers a compelling vision of what ecological recovery can look like — not just protecting what remains, but bringing back what has been lost.

## 文章 49：The Permafrost Time Bomb

**难度**：C1 | **字数**：约320词 | **主题**：永冻土与气候

---

Beneath the tundra landscapes of Siberia, Canada, and Alaska lies one of the planet's most significant — and most worrying — carbon stores: permafrost. This permanently frozen ground, which underlies approximately 25% of the northern hemisphere's land surface, contains an estimated 1.5 trillion tonnes of organic carbon — roughly twice the amount currently present in the atmosphere.

For thousands of years, this organic material — the remains of plants and animals frozen before they could decompose — has been locked in place by sub-zero temperatures. As the Arctic warms at approximately four times the global average rate, this frozen storage is beginning to thaw.

When permafrost thaws, the organic matter it contains begins to decompose, releasing carbon dioxide and methane — a greenhouse gas approximately 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a 20-year period. This creates a feedback loop: warming causes permafrost to thaw, which releases greenhouse gases, which cause more warming, which causes more permafrost thaw. Scientists call this a "positive feedback" — not positive in the sense of beneficial, but in the sense of self-reinforcing.

The critical uncertainty is the rate at which this process will unfold. Some studies suggest that permafrost thaw could already be releasing significant quantities of greenhouse gases — potentially adding the equivalent of a major industrial nation's emissions to the atmosphere. Other researchers argue that the process will be slower and that some carbon will be absorbed by expanding vegetation in newly unfrozen soils.

What is clear is that permafrost thaw represents a tipping point that is largely beyond direct human control — once triggered at sufficient scale, it would continue regardless of human emissions reductions. This is one of the key reasons climate scientists argue that the window for effective action on climate change is both narrow and rapidly closing.

## 文章 50：Living With Wildlife: Human-Animal Conflict

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约310词 | **主题**：人与野生动物共存

---

As the human population grows and expands into previously wild landscapes, the frontier between human settlement and wildlife habitat shrinks. The result is an increase in what conservationists call "human-wildlife conflict" — encounters in which humans, livestock, or crops come into conflict with wild animals, often with fatal consequences on both sides.

In sub-Saharan Africa, elephants are among the most significant sources of conflict. A single elephant can destroy an entire season's worth of crops in a single night, pushing farming families into poverty. Lions and leopards prey on livestock, and retaliatory killings by farmers — setting poison in carcasses, hunting — have contributed to dramatic declines in predator populations.

In Asia, similar dynamics play out between communities and tigers, snow leopards, and Asian elephants. In North America and Europe, the recovery of wolf and bear populations — testament to successful conservation — has brought renewed conflict with agricultural communities.

The consequences of conflict extend in both directions. For rural communities, often living in poverty and with limited insurance options, the loss of crops or livestock to wildlife can be catastrophic. The resentment this generates undermines support for conservation — if wildlife brings only costs and no benefits, local communities have little incentive to protect it.

A range of interventions are being tested. Compensation schemes — where governments pay farmers for losses caused by wildlife — can reduce retaliatory killing, though poorly designed schemes often fail. Better physical protection — predator-proof corrals for livestock, solar-powered lights to deter nocturnal wildlife, elephant-proof crop barriers — have shown promise. Community benefit-sharing from wildlife tourism — ensuring that local people receive a meaningful share of the economic value created by wildlife — can transform communities from adversaries to advocates for conservation.

Coexisting with wildlife in a shared world requires not just technical solutions, but genuine recognition of the costs borne by communities who live with nature's most dramatic animals.
