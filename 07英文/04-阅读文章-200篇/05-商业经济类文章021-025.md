## 文章 21：The Startup Revolution

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约340词 | **主题**：创业文化

---

In the late 20th century, the conventional path to business success was well-established: obtain qualifications, join a large company, work your way up over decades, and perhaps eventually reach senior management. That pathway still exists — but alongside it has emerged a radically different model: the startup.

The term "startup" refers to a newly founded company designed to grow rapidly, typically by leveraging technology to address a large market. The modern startup culture is largely associated with Silicon Valley, but it has spread to cities worldwide — from London and Berlin to Bangalore and Beijing.

The allure of the startup is powerful. The prospect of building something new, the potential for rapid wealth creation if the company succeeds, the flat hierarchies, flexible hours, and vibrant working culture — these make startups intensely attractive to ambitious young professionals. For many, the startup represents the ultimate entrepreneurial dream.

The reality, however, is demanding. Research consistently shows that roughly 90% of startups fail — most within the first five years. The causes are varied: running out of funding, failing to find product-market fit, competition from established players, or simply poor execution.

For those that succeed, the rewards can be extraordinary. Companies like Airbnb, Uber, and Spotify grew from early-stage startups to global enterprises worth billions of dollars within a decade. They did so by identifying unmet needs, deploying technology to address them at scale, and raising capital from venture capitalists willing to bet on their potential.

The startup ecosystem depends heavily on venture capital — investors who provide funding in exchange for equity stakes in early-stage companies, hoping that a small number of successes will generate returns large enough to offset the inevitable failures.

Critics argue that startup culture can be exploitative — demanding extreme hours, offering equity rather than fair wages, and celebrating a few spectacular winners while glossing over the many casualties. The romantic narrative of the visionary entrepreneur deserves scrutiny alongside its celebration.

## 文章 22：Understanding Inflation

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约320词 | **主题**：通货膨胀

---

Few economic phenomena are as directly felt in everyday life as inflation. Whether at the petrol station, the supermarket checkout, or the landlord's annual rent review, the experience of prices rising is familiar to almost everyone. But what actually causes inflation — and what can be done about it?

Inflation refers to a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over time. It is typically measured by tracking the price of a representative "basket" of goods and services — the Consumer Price Index (CPI). A moderate rate of inflation — most central banks target around 2% annually — is generally considered healthy, signalling a growing economy. It is when inflation rises significantly above this target that problems arise.

The causes of inflation are debated by economists, but several key mechanisms are widely recognised. "Demand-pull" inflation occurs when consumer demand outstrips the supply of goods and services — too much money chasing too few goods. This typically happens during periods of strong economic growth or when governments inject large amounts of money into the economy through stimulus spending. "Cost-push" inflation arises from rising production costs — wages, raw materials, or energy — that producers pass on to consumers through higher prices. Supply chain disruptions, like those triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic, can cause this type of inflation.

The primary tool central banks use to combat high inflation is raising interest rates. Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, reducing consumer spending and business investment, and thereby cooling demand. However, this medicine comes with side effects: higher rates also slow economic growth and can push vulnerable borrowers towards financial distress.

The experience of high inflation in 2021-2023, following pandemic disruptions and the energy price shock triggered by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, reminded a generation that had grown up with low inflation that price stability is not to be taken for granted.

## 文章 23：The Gig Economy: Freedom or Exploitation?

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约340词 | **主题**：零工经济

---

The growth of app-based platforms has created a new category of work that defies traditional employment categories. Uber drivers, Deliveroo cyclists, TaskRabbit handymen, and Airbnb hosts are all participants in what has come to be called the "gig economy" — a labour market characterised by short-term, flexible, project-based work rather than stable, long-term employment.

Proponents celebrate the gig economy as a revolution in worker autonomy. In this narrative, the smartphone-enabled freelancer is liberated from the rigid structures of conventional employment — free to choose when, where, and how much they work, to pursue multiple income streams simultaneously, and to fit work around personal commitments.

The reality for many gig workers is considerably less rosy. Because platform workers are typically classified as independent contractors rather than employees, they are not entitled to the employment rights that traditional workers take for granted: minimum wage guarantees, paid holidays, sick pay, pension contributions, and protection from unfair dismissal. When demand is low or the algorithm assigns fewer jobs, income can be unpredictable and inadequate.

The power asymmetry between platform companies and workers is significant. Workers must accept the terms dictated by the platform — pricing, rules, rating systems — or risk deactivation, which effectively amounts to dismissal without recourse. A poor rating from a single customer can have a disproportionate impact on a worker's livelihood.

Legal battles over the status of gig workers have been fought in courts around the world. A landmark UK Supreme Court ruling in 2021 found that Uber drivers were entitled to worker status — and therefore minimum wage and holiday pay — rather than being classified as independent contractors. Similar decisions have followed in other jurisdictions.

The debate ultimately reflects deeper questions about the nature of work in the digital age and the responsibility of corporations towards those whose labour enables their profits. Technology can be designed to empower workers or to extract maximum value from them; the choice is not inevitable but political.

## 文章 24：The Art of Negotiation

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约300词 | **主题**：谈判技巧

---

Negotiation is one of the most fundamental and frequently used skills in professional and personal life — yet it is rarely taught systematically. Whether you are negotiating a salary, a business contract, a price, or simply deciding where to eat dinner, the principles of effective negotiation determine whether you achieve your goals or leave value on the table.

Research on negotiation, much of it pioneered at Harvard's Program on Negotiation, has challenged the assumption that negotiation is a zero-sum game — where one party wins and the other loses. In most negotiations, there are opportunities to create value rather than merely divide it, if parties are willing to move beyond rigid positional bargaining.

The concept of "interests versus positions" is central to principled negotiation. A position is what someone says they want ("I want a 20% pay rise"). The underlying interest is why they want it ("I want to be recognised for my contribution and earn enough to afford a house"). Understanding the interests behind stated positions often reveals solutions that satisfy both parties better than either's opening position.

Preparation is the most underrated aspect of negotiation. Knowing your BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) — what you will do if the negotiation fails — determines your walk-away point and therefore your leverage. The stronger your BATNA, the stronger your negotiating position.

Active listening and asking open questions are powerful negotiating tools. "What is most important to you in this deal?" or "Help me understand the constraints you're working with" can reveal information that makes a better agreement possible.

Finally, how a negotiation ends matters as much as the outcome itself. Agreements that both parties feel are fair tend to be honoured; those that feel coerced tend to create resentment and resistance.

## 文章 25：Globalisation: Opportunity and Backlash

**难度**：C1 | **字数**：约360词 | **主题**：全球化与反全球化

---

For much of the late 20th century, globalisation was the dominant paradigm in economic thinking — a largely unquestioned consensus that the integration of national economies through trade, investment, and the movement of people was beneficial for all. Today, that consensus is under serious challenge.

The gains from globalisation were real and significant. The opening of global markets lifted hundreds of millions of people out of poverty, particularly in East and South Asia. Trade allowed countries to specialise in producing goods where they had comparative advantages, reducing costs and increasing overall efficiency. Global supply chains became extraordinarily sophisticated, enabling consumer goods to be produced at previously unimaginable scales and prices.

But the benefits were unevenly distributed. While globalisation raised overall living standards and benefited consumers through lower prices, it also contributed to the hollowing out of manufacturing in advanced economies, concentrating its effects on communities dependent on industries like steel, textiles, and electronics. Workers in these sectors saw their jobs disappear or their wages stagnate. The communities that bore these costs were often geographically and politically marginalised.

The political consequences have been profound. Rising economic anxiety, combined with cultural concerns about immigration and a perception that elites were benefiting while working people suffered, fuelled the wave of populism that swept through many Western democracies in the 2010s. Brexit, the election of Donald Trump, and the rise of nationalist parties across Europe can all be at least partly understood as reactions to the perceived failures of globalisation.

The COVID-19 pandemic and the geopolitical shock of Russia's invasion of Ukraine revealed how dependent economies had become on complex global supply chains — and how vulnerable these were to disruption. Governments began talking of "reshoring" and "friend-shoring" — moving supply chains closer to home or to trusted allied nations.

The future of globalisation is unlikely to be complete reversal, but a more managed, selective, and politically conscious form of international economic integration — one that takes seriously both the opportunities and the obligations that interconnection creates.
