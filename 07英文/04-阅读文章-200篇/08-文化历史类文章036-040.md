## 文章 36：The Printing Press and the Protestant Reformation

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约310词 | **主题**：活字印刷与宗教改革

---

Few inventions have changed human history as dramatically as the printing press. When Johannes Gutenberg completed his press in Mainz around 1440, he could not have known that he was creating a technology that would undermine the authority of the Catholic Church, spark a religious revolution, and fundamentally alter the nature of European civilisation.

Before the printing press, books were produced by hand — a laborious process that made them extremely expensive and restricted their ownership to the Church, the aristocracy, and wealthy scholars. The Church controlled access to the Bible itself: it was written in Latin, a language ordinary people could not read, and only authorised clergy could interpret its meaning.

Gutenberg's press changed everything. By 1500, over 20 million books had been printed in Europe. The cost of a book fell by approximately 99%. For the first time in history, ideas could spread with a speed and reach that no authority could control.

When Martin Luther nailed his 95 Theses to the church door in Wittenberg in 1517, challenging the corruption of Catholic Church practices like the sale of indulgences, he could not have caused more than a local controversy without the printing press. Within two weeks, his theses had been printed and distributed throughout Germany. Within months, they had spread across Europe.

The Reformation — the religious revolution that Luther's challenge sparked — fractured the unity of Western Christianity and launched centuries of religious conflict and war. But it also established new principles that would eventually contribute to the development of religious freedom, democratic governance, and the individual's right to interpret texts for themselves.

The printing press did not cause the Reformation alone, but it was the essential infrastructure that made a movement of ideas unstoppable.

## 文章 37：Food and Cultural Identity

**难度**：B1-B2 | **字数**：约280词 | **主题**：食物与文化认同

---

Of all the ways humans express cultural identity — language, clothing, architecture, music — food may be the most intimate. What we eat, how we prepare it, and the rituals surrounding meals connect us to our families, our communities, and our histories in ways that are often more visceral than conscious.

Every culture has developed a distinctive food culture shaped by its geography, climate, religion, and history. Japanese cuisine celebrates seasonal ingredients and precise technique; Italian food places extraordinary value on regional simplicity and the quality of a few well-chosen ingredients; Indian cooking reflects thousands of years of spice trade history and the vegetarian traditions of Hindu and Jain communities.

Food has also been a vehicle for cultural exchange. The "Columbian Exchange" — the transfer of plants, animals, and foods between the Americas and the Old World following Columbus's voyages — fundamentally transformed food cultures on both sides of the Atlantic. Tomatoes, potatoes, maize, chilli, chocolate, and vanilla all originated in the Americas; European colonisers brought wheat, rice, cattle, and pigs westward. It is now impossible to imagine Italian cuisine without tomatoes or Indian cuisine without chilli — both relatively recent arrivals from the Americas.

In immigrant communities, food often becomes one of the most powerful connections to a homeland left behind. The smell of a parent's cooking can transport a person to childhood and connection across thousands of miles.

Yet food cultures are not static; they evolve through contact, migration, and creativity. "Fusion cuisine" — the blending of culinary traditions — is both a commercial trend and a reflection of the fact that food has always been one of the most natural sites of cultural exchange.

## 文章 38：The Art of Translation

**难度**：B2 | **字数**：约300词 | **主题**：翻译的艺术

---

When the Italian poet Dante wrote the Divine Comedy in the early 14th century, he chose to write in Italian rather than Latin — an act of linguistic defiance that helped establish Italian as a literary language. Today, Dante is read worldwide, in languages he could never have imagined, by readers who depend on translators to bridge the chasm of language and time.

Translation is one of the most intellectually demanding and culturally significant of human activities, yet it rarely receives the recognition it deserves. Every translation involves not just the transfer of meaning from one language to another, but a series of interpretive choices — about tone, cultural reference, the balance between fidelity and readability, and the translator's understanding of what the original text is really trying to do.

The challenge is that languages are not parallel systems — they encode reality differently. Some languages have words for experiences that others lack entirely. The Portuguese concept of "saudade" — a melancholic longing for something absent — has no precise English equivalent. The German "schadenfreude" — pleasure at another's misfortune — has been borrowed directly into English because no English word quite captures it.

Literary translation raises these challenges most acutely. A poem's meaning cannot be separated from its sound, rhythm, and form — properties that are specific to a language and cannot simply be transferred. Great translations are therefore also creative works in their own right.

The stakes of translation are not only literary. Diplomatic communication, international law, scientific publishing, and humanitarian assistance all depend on accurate, culturally informed translation. The consequences of mistranslation in high-stakes diplomatic contexts can be serious indeed.

The best translators are invisible — their work so transparent that readers feel they are encountering the original voice directly, while the translator's skill works silently behind the scenes.

## 文章 39：The History of the Olympic Games

**难度**：B1-B2 | **字数**：约300词 | **主题**：奥运会历史

---

Every four years, athletes from around the world gather to compete under a shared ideal: that excellence in human physical achievement transcends nationality, politics, and conflict. The modern Olympic Games are one of the world's most powerful symbols of international cooperation — but their history is more complicated than their ideals suggest.

The ancient Olympic Games were held in Olympia, Greece, from approximately 776 BCE until 393 CE — nearly 1,200 years. They were a religious festival honouring Zeus as much as a sporting competition, held every four years and attracting competitors from across the Greek world. A truce — the "Olympic Truce" — was declared during the games, allowing athletes and spectators to travel safely.

The modern Olympic Games were revived in 1896 in Athens by Pierre de Coubertin, a French educator who believed that athletic competition could promote international understanding and world peace. The first modern Games attracted 241 athletes from 14 nations competing in 43 events.

The Olympics grew rapidly, but their history has been punctuated by controversy. The 1936 Berlin Games under the Nazi regime were controversially used for propaganda, though Jesse Owens' four gold medals delivered a powerful counter-narrative. The 1972 Munich Games were overshadowed by the murder of eleven Israeli athletes by Palestinian terrorists. The Cold War produced boycotts of the 1980 Moscow and 1984 Los Angeles Games.

Despite — or perhaps because of — these controversies, the Olympics retain extraordinary cultural power. They provide a platform for national pride, extraordinary individual achievement, and occasional moments of transcendent sportsmanship. The ideal of peaceful competition across borders remains as relevant as ever, even if the reality often falls short of the aspiration.

## 文章 40：The Legacy of Colonialism

**难度**：C1 | **字数**：约350词 | **主题**：殖民主义的遗产

---

The colonial empires of the 15th to 20th centuries were among the most transformative — and most destructive — forces in world history. At their peak, European powers controlled approximately 84% of the earth's surface. The consequences of this domination — economic, social, cultural, and psychological — continue to shape the world today.

The economic legacy of colonialism is deeply contested. Colonial powers extracted vast quantities of natural resources — gold, silver, rubber, cotton, diamonds, oil — from their territories, often using coerced or enslaved labour. Agricultural systems were restructured to produce export crops for metropolitan markets rather than food for local populations. The infrastructure built — railways, ports, telegraph lines — was designed to serve extraction rather than development. Economists like Utsa Patnaik have argued that Britain extracted enormous wealth from India over the colonial period.

Cultural legacies are equally complex. Colonial rule involved the systematic devaluation of indigenous languages, religions, and cultural practices, and the imposition of European languages, religions, and administrative systems. In many colonised societies, these disruptions severed connections to pre-colonial traditions and created lasting identity crises. Post-colonial literature — from Chinua Achebe to Frantz Fanon to Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie — has been centrally concerned with recovering and renegotiating these disrupted identities.

The political boundaries drawn by colonial powers — often arbitrarily, without regard for ethnic, cultural, or historical realities — continue to cause conflict across Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia. Many of the world's most intractable conflicts have their roots in colonial boundaries and the power vacuums left by decolonisation.

Conversations about colonialism's legacy have intensified in recent years, including debates about reparations, the repatriation of cultural objects held in European museums, and the inclusion of colonial history in national education curricula. These debates are uncomfortable for former colonial powers, but engaging with them honestly is essential to understanding the contemporary world.
